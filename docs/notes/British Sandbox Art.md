## Evidence and chronological boundaries

**Worker housing and ordinary materials.** Historic England's
[Isleworth pottery record](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1435957)
documents a small works founded in 1756–1757 and worker cottages shown on the
1813 enclosure map. Its insurance records describe modest 1765–1786 premises,
clay/sand/coal stores and kilns. This supplies a dated production/worker-housing
context, not our precise terrace elevation. The site's illustrated 1829 view
is outside the game window and is not copied as an original 1813 roofline.
Brick, slate, pale trim, timber sash and chimneys use the wider Georgian
vernacular described by the [Bath Preservation Trust's conservation guide](https://www.bath-preservation-trust.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Warmer-Bath.pdf).
Bath's local stone is not evidence that every worker house was red brick; the
paired brick terrace is original composite art, with its own floor plan and
proportions. It is not a palace-fronted Royal Crescent or Victorian back-to-back.

**Civic/market type.** Historic England's
[commerce and exchange guide](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/dlsg-commerce-exchange-buildings/heag121-commerce-and-exchange-lsg/)
describes market/exchange functions and eighteenth-century arcaded ranges,
including Halifax's Piece Hall. Our small brick assembly range above open
square-pier stalls is an interpretation of that commercial vocabulary; its
clock, counters, two roofs and civic-research role are invented. The 1830s/1860s
corn exchanges illustrated in the guide are excluded. No Victorian glazed
train-shed, iron market canopy or Gothic municipal palace is used by default.

**Ceramic production.** Historic England's archaeology guide,
[Pottery Production Sites](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/archaeological-and-historic-pottery-production-sites/heag019-pottery-production-sites/),
dates the development of small bottle kilns to the early/middle eighteenth
century and describes material preparation/production evidence. This supports
the type; the miniature's stepped shoulders, neck, fire mouth and attached
workroom are original approximations. Coalport's founding around 1795 alone
does not establish the construction date of every surviving photographed kiln.
We therefore do not copy a Victorian bottle-kiln complex, use Gladstone's later
factory as an eighteenth-century plan, or add steam engines, railway tracks,
continuous kilns or automated production. Clay-to-pottery is the shared game
recipe; it does not simulate the many materials, firings, skills or commerce of
Georgian earthenware/porcelain manufacture.

**Barracks and infirmary.** The
[Survey of London: Woolwich, chapter 7](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/bartlett/sites/bartlett/files/sol-woolwich9-ch7.pdf)
dates military occupation from 1773, hospital construction to 1778–1780, French
Wars enlargements and the long barrack extension to 1801–1807. It also identifies
later alterations. Our short brick range/parade ground and small rear infirmary
are original compressed interpretations. The site housed artillery, whereas our
passive troop candidates are infantry comparisons; they are not asserted to
be its actual garrison. No 1854–1855 ward block, 1860s church, later Scottish
Baronial treatment or the repository's post-1815 permanent Rotunda is copied.

**Agricultural comparison.** [UK Parliament's enclosure account](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/transformingsociety/towncountry/landscape/overview/enclosingland/)
places increased parliamentary enclosure from the 1750s. It supports a bounded
arable landscape comparison, not the crop, hedge, shed or plot dimensions here.
No claim is made that this tiny permanent grain bed reconstructs a particular
farm or represents historical land rights, workforce or productivity.

## Nine role cards

and eaves stay within the declared footprint. Parts are anchored, noncolliding,
nonqueryable and script-free. World adds query/ownership targets separately.
Prices, output, capacity, recipes and research gates remain shared game budgets.

Regular sash grids, paired terrace masses/chimneys, brick/slate civic ranges,
loading doors and a small bottle kiln make a separate Georgian family. Similar
primitive helpers do not recolour the Spartan plaster house or Roman arcade.
The palette is art direction, not a claim that all British buildings used the
same local stone, brick, roofing or paint. Reused supplied farm-worker clothing,
if used by World, remains provisional rather than reviewed Georgian dress.

## Passive role candidates

- **Frontline:** red coat, plain early-nineteenth-century shako, cross-belts and
  musket/socket-bayonet-shaped props. The US National Park Service's
  [Brown Bess account](https://www.nps.gov/articles/brown-bess.htm)
  confirms the weapon's use during the Revolutionary/Napoleonic/1812 wars.
  The [Museum of the Mercian Regiment's original circa-1808–1812 stovepipe shako](https://www.stand-firm-strike-hard.org.uk/hats-throughout-history)
  supplies a dated hat comparison; the later replicas/patterns on that page are
  not treated as originals. Exact coat cut, facing colour, straps, fittings,
  simplified shako and resting grip remain interpretive, without a regimental
  identity or the claim that one uniform covered the whole 1750–1815 period.
- **Missile:** green-jacket rifleman with a shorter flintlock-shaped prop and
  cartridge box. The National Army Museum's
  [Corps of Riflemen account](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/rifle-brigade-prince-consorts-own)
  identifies its formation in 1800, green jackets and the shorter rifle; Royal
  Armouries' [Waterloo equipment discussion](https://royalarmouries.org/objects-and-stories/stories/waterloo-1815)
  distinguishes the Pattern 1800 and 1805 Baker examples. Our geometry is a
  simplified candidate, not an exact weapon model, specific unit portrait or
  later Victorian rifle/helmet. Rifleman terminology follows the selected early
  nineteenth-century phase, not the regiment's later honorific name.
- **Support:** dark coat, simple cocked hat, wooden medical case and rolled
  linen. The National Archives' [James Barry career record](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/teaching-resources/career-of-dr-james-barry/)
  documents an army hospital-assistant appointment in 1813. This establishes a
  period role, not the model's hat, uniform, portable kit or Barry's likeness.
  Those remain provisional. No later Red Cross, post-1815 stethoscope, modern
  medical uniform or promise of healing efficacy is introduced.

These original figures have no Humanoid, Tool, scripts, combat registration,
damage/healing stats, muzzle effects, projectiles or firing/attack behavior.
The firearm-shaped parts are passive scenery. BodyColors/skin and warrior face
match the existing British NPC art default, a fictional palette rather than a
population-wide claim. Original rigs/equipment templates are never mutated.

## API, verification and historical limits

`BritishKit.available(kind)`, `create(kind)` and `createActor(role)` match the
other kits: unparented floor-origin models, scale 0.25, `CivilizationId=britain`
and role/evidence attributes. World owns interactions, ownership, roster and
workers. No profile schema, balances, World/mapping or production change is
part of this delivery.

`tests/miniature-game/BritainScene.server.luau` waits up to 15 seconds for the
ready injected-fake boundary. It checks all nine building and three actor
roles, native footprint/floor, scale, four cardinal rotations, inert flags,
retained body/equipment poses, source fingerprints and geometry different from
Sparta/Rome. It reports `MiniatureBritainSceneResult` and destroys temporary
models without selecting a civilization or writing player state.
