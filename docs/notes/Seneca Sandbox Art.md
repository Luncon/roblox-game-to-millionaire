# Seneca sandbox art evidence

October 29, 2026. Original native geometry for the Seneca identity, **1600–1680
CE**, in the separate fake-store sandbox. This kit uses Seneca-specific heritage
interpretation alongside explicitly identified archaeological comparisons. It is
a set of game designs, not a reconstruction of one excavated town. Read with
[[Civilization Building and Army Reference]], [[Miniature Civilization Redesign]]
and [[Miniature Economy Implementation]].

## Evidence cards

- **Seneca dwelling structure:** [Ganondagan's Bark Longhouse](https://www.ganondagan.org/bark-longhouse)
  documents its reconstruction furnished for about 1670: elm framing, elm-bark
  covering, fiber lashings, end doors, roof smoke openings and interior storage
  platforms. Our curved, segmented bark shell, visible lashings and roof opening
  interpret that construction. Footprints compress the proportions considerably;
  the game house does not imply a historically tiny single-family longhouse.
  The civic hall uses the same construction vocabulary with a longer axis and
  an outdoor meeting/trade area, an invented gameplay arrangement.
- **Living Seneca interpretation:** Ganondagan's
  [Life of the Seneca program](https://www.ganondagan.org/educational-outreach/life-of-the-seneca)
  specifically treats the 1600s and includes bone tools, cornhusk items, wooden
  bowls and colonial trade goods. Everyday materials guide the work areas.
  The community's history is not represented as an isolated, permanently
  pre-contact culture. The kit's small prop selection omits much documented
  seventeenth-century trade equipment rather than claiming it was absent.
- **Food:** [Ganondagan's Three Sisters account](https://www.ganondagan.org/plants-1/things-that-sustain-us,-the-three-sisters)
  identifies corn, beans and squash as central Seneca crops and describes stored
  corn and beans at Fort Hill. The farm therefore shows all three crops on low
  planting mounds, rather than a European wheat field. Plant spacing, maturity,
  color and the compressed planting layout are invented. Ganondagan's
  [White Corn Project](https://www.ganondagan.org/white-corn-project) also makes
  clear that this food tradition continues in living Haudenosaunee communities.
- **Local work and storage comparison:** the Rochester Museum & Science Center's
  [Extending the House scene](https://rmsc.org/exhibits/haudenosaunee-continuity/)
  interprets an Onödowa'ga:' (Seneca) village near Honeoye Creek about 1600. It
  includes pole/bark construction, hickory lashings, hand-shaped pots, drying
  surfaces, bow hunting and elevated corn storage. Our open timber work area,
  artisan mat and raised bark store interpret these everyday activities. This
  is a museum interpretation, not direct proof of each model's exact plan.
  The storage roof, target markers and detached work shelters remain original
  abstractions. No image or museum model is copied into the game.
- **Pottery chronology:** Gian Carlo Cervone's primary collection study,
  [“Seneca Pottery Analysis,” NYSAA Bulletin 95, 1987, pp. 23–31](https://nysarchaeology.org/download/nysaa/bulletin/number_095.pdf),
  examines the historic Seneca sequence and notes that native pottery becomes
  extremely rare by 1687. Our clay-to-pottery ledger therefore represents an
  **early-contact craft abstraction within the broader identity period**, not
  a claim that every late-seventeenth-century Seneca settlement ran a pottery
  industry. The open firing bed is interpretive, not an excavated kiln plan.
  Generic unmarked vessels avoid assigning clan or ceremonial designs.

The NYS Museum's [Garoga pottery research](https://nysm.nysed.gov/research-collections/archaeology/native-american-archaeology/news/mohawk-garoga-pottery)
concerns a **Mohawk**, sixteenth-century site. It is a useful comparative
Haudenosaunee study, but does not certify Seneca forms or our chosen century.
The Smithsonian's [probably Seneca jar record](https://americanindian.si.edu/collections-search/object/ark:/65665/ws60d140e24f7074448837f7ce101e203fa)
has an earlier probable date and a withheld image; that image was not accessed or
used to reproduce a vessel. These distinctions prevent a generic Haudenosaunee
comparison being presented as direct Seneca evidence.

## Nine-role manifest

The catalog footprints, prices, outputs, worker slots and research effects are
unchanged. Food remains the shared Food ledger; the clay/pottery recipe keeps the
same explicitly fictional production and sale budget. Drying clay and crop props
do not add new goods or crafting bonuses.

| Kind / footprint | Distinct geometry | Interpretation and remaining limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `civic` · 10 × 8 | Long bark hall across the footprint, arched shell, end entrances, meeting clearing, log seating and trade mat | Ganondagan construction vocabulary; combined public/trade function and plan invented. No clan authority or ceremonial institution is assigned by the model. |
| `house` · 6 × 6 | Narrow bark dwelling with many curved roof chords, fiber lashings, roof opening, interior platforms and entry tray | Seneca longhouse construction; significantly compressed proportions and housing capacity are game abstractions. |
| `farm` · 8 × 8 | Fifteen low mounds with maize stalks/ears, bean vines and squash | Seneca food reference; seasonal growth, planting spacing and fixed mature appearance invented. |
| `timber` · 8 × 6 | Open pole stock, bark shade, working support, unfinished wooden tray and stone-adze abstraction | Museum work comparison; exact tool, timber species and supply-yard arrangement require closer review. |
| `material` · 8 × 6 | Low clay collection bed, plain work mat and separated clay portions | Clay availability is a game supply role. Exact extraction, tempering and transport procedures are not reconstructed. |
| `workshop` · 8 × 6 | Small bark shade, shaping mat, plain vessels, drying stone and open wood firing bed | Early-contact craft interpretation; dates and changing pottery use are qualified above. No European masonry kiln is substituted. |
| `storehouse` · 8 × 6 | Elevated bark store with supporting poles, ladder and hanging corn | Museum storage comparison; rectangular flat cover and compact plan simplify the source interpretation. Not every Seneca store had this form. |
| `training` · 10 × 8 | Open practice clearing, partial bark shade, wooden markers and bow rest | Bow-use vocabulary with invented military training site. Targets and dedicated building are not an identified Seneca institution. |
| `healer` · 6 × 6 | Small bark shelter, resting mat, plain supply bundle and vessel | Explicit gameplay care role. Dedicated medical building, uniform and equipment remain unverified. No healing functionality is added. |

The curved bark shells, exposed framing, lashings and smoke openings distinguish
these dwellings from Norse timber halls with pitched roofs. Other work roles
remain open work areas or purpose-specific stores instead of receiving the same
longhouse model. Totem poles, Plains tipis, invented clan emblems, sacred objects
and later pan-Indigenous regalia are absent from this everyday architecture kit.

## Passive role candidates

| Role | Visible design | Evidence and limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `melee` | Plain wooden club, hide-colored upper garment, waist wrap, leggings and moccasins | A broad northeastern frontline candidate. RMSC's earlier c.1400 interior includes a club, but this does not establish the exact c.1600–1680 Seneca head shape or dress. The plain ball-ended prop remains provisional. |
| `ranged` | Resting wooden bow, string and plain arrow case, with the same everyday clothing vocabulary | RMSC's c.1600 Seneca village scene includes a hunter carrying a bow and arrow case. Hunting evidence does not establish a standard military uniform or complete battlefield equipment. |
| `medic` | Plain supply bag and folded cloth, with no weapon or medical insignia | Gameplay support candidate. This costume and permanent specialist slot are unverified; medicinal knowledge is not reduced to a reconstructed recipe or sacred object. |

Clothing cuts, garment coverage, colors and tied hair are restrained artistic
choices, not certified reconstructions. No feather bonnet, face paint or clan
identifier is assigned. The skin palette is an art default, not a statement about
every historical Seneca person. BodyColors and body-part colors agree and the
existing project warrior face is retained. These original figures contain no
Humanoid, Tool, scripts, combat registration or healing behavior. They do not
replace the separate worker renderer; its supplied farmer clothing is still
provisional and not Seneca clothing evidence. Farming roles must not be read as
a historical claim about who tended the crops.

## Implementation and verification boundary

`SenecaKit.create(kind)`, `available(kind)` and `createActor(role)` match the shared
kit interface. Models are authored at full size and scaled once to **0.25**.
All scenery and role parts are anchored, noncolliding, nontouching and
nonqueryable. Each model links this note. No imported model or source template is
edited.

`tests/miniature-game/SenecaScene.server.luau` creates temporary unparented models,
checks all nine roles and three passive figures, all part corners within their
footprints at four cardinal rotations, a bounded part count, bark-arch/lashing
structure, crop/storage props, actor equipment, body colors and inert behavior,
then destroys the temporary models. It reports
`SenecaScenePassed/SenecaSceneResult` in the fake-store Studio test place.

Pinned StyLua and Selene pass for the kit and fixture.
